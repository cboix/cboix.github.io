I am a computational biologist and postdoctoral research fellow in the Department of Biomedical Informatics at Harvard Medical School, working with Shamil Sunyaev and supported by an NIA K99/R00 Pathway to Independence award.

I develop statistical genetics methods, mechanistic interpretability tools for AI models, and genomic resources to study the molecular basis of human disease, with a focus on neurodegeneration. My research interests include:

  • Partitioning traits into molecular mechanisms. Developing methods that use the shared effects of genetic variants across traits to identify molecular pathways underlying complex disease, model disease heterogeneity, and find genes whose loss protects against disease.
  • Sequence-level mechanisms of variation. Dissecting how variants alter DNA, RNA, and protein function, from biophysical effects to gene regulation in specific cellular contexts, both individually and in combination.
  • Learning new biology from sequence models. Building tools to interpret biological sequence models and using these models to understand the molecular effects of variants and their interactions.
  • 3′UTRs in neuronal function. Mapping how alternative polyadenylation shapes mRNA localization and local translation in neurons, identifying the sequence determinants of these downstream functions of 3′UTRs, and understanding how these processes are disrupted in neurodegeneration.
  • Selective neuronal vulnerability. Identifying the molecular mechanisms that make specific neuronal populations vulnerable in Parkinson's and Alzheimer's disease, and how genetic risk converges on these cell types.

Before this, I received my Ph.D. in Computational and Systems Biology from MIT with Manolis Kellis. There, I built EpiMap, linking disease loci to the tissues and enhancer programs through which they act, and developed methods for atlas-scale single-cell data. With Li-Huei Tsai's group, I co-led single-cell studies of more than 400 aged and Alzheimer's brains, identified selectively vulnerable neurons, and helped map how DNA double-strand breaks disrupt neuronal genome organization in AD.

I received my undergraduate degree from Princeton University, where I worked with John Storey on statistics for high-dimensional genomic data.
